# Strength × running — judging the combined week

This note grounds the *weekly* verdict: how strength work (muscu) and running fit together
for an endurance-first athlete, and how to tell a good week from a poor one when both are in
play. Running is the goal; strength is a support, not a competing objective.

## The priority order
- **The run goal comes first.** Strength serves running — durability, running economy, injury
  resilience, force at the end of a race — it is not a separate objective to maximise. Never
  trade running quality or long-run volume for tonnage.
- Good strength for a runner is **consistent and modest** (typically 2–3 short sessions/week):
  enough to build resilience, not so much that it steals recovery from the runs that matter.

## Concurrent-training interference (the core mechanism)
- Endurance and heavy strength send partly opposing adaptation signals. In practice the
  interference that matters is **recovery competition**: heavy lower-body work leaves legs
  fatigued and sore for 24–72 h, which blunts the *quality* and *long* runs that drive the
  goal.
- So the judgement is about **placement and legs**, not total volume:
  - Heavy leg sessions (squat/deadlift/lunges — QUADS/HAMSTRINGS/GLUTES/CALVES) should sit
    **away from key running days** (quality sessions, long run) — ideally 24–48 h before them,
    or on the same day *after* an easy run, never the day before a hard run.
  - Upper-body / core work interferes little with running and can go almost anywhere.
- A week with heavy legs the day before every quality run is a **poorly sequenced week**, even
  if the numbers look fine.

## What a good combined week looks like
- The planned runs were done, easy/hard balance (~80/20) respected, and strength happened
  **without compromising** the quality session or the long run.
- Strength was regular (not a single random session, not five), leg load was placed sensibly,
  and no single muscle group is being chronically neglected *within the runner's modest scope*
  (don't push hypertrophy balance at the cost of running).
- Body weight is stable or drifting gently in the intended direction.

## Cross-modal fatigue — the red flags
- **Double spike:** running load already high (acute:chronic > ~1.4, or form/TSB deeply
  negative) **and** strength volume also jumped → too much total stress. Back one off — almost
  always **cut the muscu first**, protect the running.
- **Legs never fresh:** heavy leg sessions stacked against quality/long runs, so runs are
  always run on tired legs → the running goal is being sabotaged by the strength schedule.
- **Under-fuelling risk (RED-S):** high combined load *plus* a sharp unintended weight drop, or
  low energy/heavy legs in the readiness check-in → flag it; more training on an energy deficit
  is how runners get hurt or sick. This overrides "do more".
- Any single-set red flag from the load-and-recovery note (sharp/localised pain, pain at rest)
  still trumps everything — rest and screen, never coach through it.

## How to phrase the verdict
- Judge the **week as a whole**: is the running on track for the goal, and is the strength
  supporting it or getting in its way?
- Give one clear headline (good week / hold / rebalance / back off), the one or two reasons
  that matter, then the concrete change for next week.
- Running changes can be specific (a session swap). **Strength changes are advice only** in this
  app (there is no planned muscu programme to rewrite): say *what* to change (frequency, leg-day
  placement, drop a session), not a rewritten plan.
